Chapter 1. The Day the Water Would Not Answer

Seria fell over three times on the sand.

The first time, it was the wave's fault.
The second time, the sand gave way.
The third time, she had no idea who to blame.

Her legs splayed in opposite directions. Cold water washed between them and drained away.

Lying on her stomach, she glared at her feet.

Ten pale, slender things stuck out from the ends. When she tensed them, they curled up without permission.

Toes. She knew the word.

Until a little while ago, she had not known what they actually did.

Seria pressed her palms into the sand and straightened her elbows. Her shoulders and back, strong from swimming, lifted her upper body easily enough. The trouble lay below them, in the legs she had only just acquired.

If I were underwater, all this would take is one flick of my tail…

This time, her knees buckled inward. The harder she tried to stand, the more they folded, until she sat down again with a thump.

A gull some distance away tilted its head, as if it had expected her to drop a fish.

“I don't have any.”

The gull stayed where it was.

“There's nothing here for you.”

It took a step closer.

Seria flung a handful of wet sand. The gull took flight, making a point of circling her head once before it left.

The waxed pouch at her waist was still safe. She slipped a hand beneath the wet fabric of her clothes and felt the outline of the flat object inside.

A cracked shard of black ceramic.

She had found it among dead coral on the seabed. Grooves ran beneath a crust of black residue, and a faint mark remained on one side.

She did not know what it was for. She had never seen anything like it in the waters of her home.

The young fish around it had been struggling to swim. She had cupped one that was listing on its side and carried it to a clean current.

When she opened her hands, it did not move.

That day, her eldest sister had closed the gate to the southern spawning grounds. The black water had spread inside, forcing out even the people tending the eggs and young fish.

By the time Seria left home, black threads had caught in the first garden beneath the royal palace.

She took her hand off the pouch.

A harbor lay at the far end of the shore. Masts crowded behind a long stone breakwater.

Above them stood a tower.

Even in the morning sun, its gray walls looked dark and wet. Its top disappeared into low cloud.

The Returnflow Tower of Bellu.

That was what the seafaring merchants called it. Now that she was above the surface and could see it for herself, she thought even the palace's tallest coral pillar would look like a toothpick beside it.

Orna had told her that those who followed the black water upstream had turned back off Bellu. Beyond that point lay the harbor's drainage works. The tower managed the waterways; someone there ought to have records.

Seria did not yet know whether the shard in her pouch had washed out from that place. But she knew where to start asking.

She had to get there.

* * *

At the palace, they were still choosing an envoy to send ashore.

Who should go? Whom should they approach when they reached the harbor?

In the time it took to hold two meetings, three more spawning grounds had closed.

Seria had not waited for a third.

She had left a letter promising to find out where the black shard came from and return, then gone to Orna.

Crossing the sea was easy. What came afterward was the problem.

Orna had a potion that could turn a tail into legs. There was only one dose ready.

Before handing it over, she had made Seria promise more than once.

“When you come back, you come to me first. Before we turn you back, I need to see what the change has done to your body.”

Seria had promised.

The pair of pearls in her ears ought to pay for food and a bed for a few days. Surely it would not take any longer than that to ask where a broken piece of ceramic had come from.

Yet here she was on land, unable to manage more than a few steps across a beach.

She drew her right foot in close and planted it on the sand, then brought the left beside it.

This time, she took it slowly. With both hands braced on the ground, she lifted her hips and eased her weight onto her feet.

Her legs trembled when she took her hands away.

But she was standing.

Just as she registered that small victory, a wave struck her heels. She flung her arms wide and barely kept her balance.

First, she checked whether anyone had seen.

The gull was gone.

Good.

She steadied herself on her right foot and carefully moved her left. Only after she felt the sole touch the sand did she shift her weight onto it.

Orna had drawn two feet on a rock on the seabed to explain it: hold yourself up with one foot, move the other.

Seria had said she understood perfectly.

Her left knee wobbled.

She stopped, planted both feet firmly, and waited until she had her balance before moving the right.

* * *

She had no idea how long it took to reach the harbor.

By the time the sun began to warm her cheek, she was standing at the foot of a low flight of stone steps.

Walking was one thing. Stairs were another.

She had to set a foot on the step, then use that leg to pull the rest of herself up.

Seria leaned on the wall beside her. Grit came away under her fingers. The sea wind had worn even the edges of the stones smooth.

On the fifth step, something stung the sole of her right foot.

She propped herself against the wall and lifted it. A tiny piece of shell was embedded in the skin. When she picked it out with a fingernail, a round bead of blood welled up.

Underwater, it would have trailed away in a thin red thread. Here, it sat on her skin.

She watched it for a moment, then lifted the hem of her clothes to wipe it off.

The cut stung when she put her foot down. Keeping a hand on the wall, she slowly climbed the next step.

People were hauling cargo at the top. A two-wheeled cart rattled past, and a man with rope over his shoulder swore at a crate blocking his way.

The smells of wet timber, fish, and something burning hit her all at once. Seria wrinkled her nose.

“Out of the way, please.”

The woman beside her had hauled a cart full of salt sacks up the slope next to the steps.

“How do I get to the Returnflow Tower?” Seria asked.

“Head for the tall thing. Move first.”

Seria looked toward the tower and stepped aside.

The woman's gaze traveled from her bare feet to her wet clothes, then stopped at the pearls in her ears.

“Fall off a ship?”

“No.”

“What, then? Walk out of the sea?”

Seria said nothing.

The woman asked no more questions. She pointed across the road with a cart handle.

“Take the stone path over there. The boards over the drain are rotten.”

Seria looked where she was pointing. A narrow channel ran beside the path, covered in places by dark, sodden planks. Nets hauled from the sea lay piled on top.

“Thank you.”

The woman adjusted her grip and pulled the cart away.

She had gone only a few steps when a bell rang.

One long peal. Then two short ones.

The people carrying cargo stopped. A red cloth unfurled on the far quay, and someone shouted.

“Get back! Leave it and run!”

The man threw the rope off his shoulder. A crate hit the stones. People shoved past one another into the alleyways.

The woman let go of her cart and ran too.

It rolled backward down the slope toward Seria. She flattened herself against the wall just before a wheel struck the stone lip beside the steps.

Clatter!

The salt sacks lurched to one side. Seria dodged the rocking cart as one sack tumbled out. Its side split against the paving, spilling white grains across the stones.

She edged around the cart with one hand on the wall. From below the steps came the smell of stagnant mud.

A thin black film was spreading across the water inside the breakwater.

Something moved beneath it.

At first, she thought it was a drifting net. Then it came toward the steps, against the current.

Click.

The pointed tip of a leg caught the edge of a step.

A body the size of a handcart rose from the water. Barnacles crusted its wet shell. One of its front claws was broken; the other was long and white.

The intact claw scraped the stone, bringing down a fall of pale dust.

A second shell surfaced behind it.

She knew these creatures. They cracked open shellfish on shallow reefs. She had seen them move to other reefs when the water turned foul.

Had they come right out of the water this time?

“Move!”

A man holding a child's hand ran toward the narrow gap beside the cart. Seria pressed against the wall to let them past.

His foot skidded on the spilled salt.

He lost the child's hand and slammed one knee into the paving. The child pitched forward too.

The first creature turned. Its sharp front legs caught the top step, and its claw opened toward the child.

The man braced one hand on the ground and reached out with the other.

“Come here!”

The claw was closing in before the child could get up.

Seria saw that the creature's hind legs were still underwater and reached out.

Drive the water upward. Tip it back down the steps.

She called to the current as she always had, reaching to catch its flow.

Her fingertips stopped in empty air.

Nothing answered.

When she called the water, a cool sensation used to spread from the small of her back to the tip of her tail. Every scale would turn, and the water would follow.

Now she had legs.

Shaking knees, and a foot cut by a piece of shell.

Once you have legs, you won't be able to command the currents.

Orna's words came back to her.

Your power over the water is in your tail. Change it into legs, and you lose the use of that power too.

She had known that when she drank the potion. Yet with a claw reaching for the child, she had fallen back on the one thing she knew how to do.

As Seria lowered her hand, the creature advanced.

The man trying to lift the child faltered at the sight of the claw.

“Take the child! Go!”

Seria shoved the side of the salt cart. It swung across the path.

She could not have said whether she had pushed it properly or simply fallen into it.

The man scooped up the child and ran into an alley.

The white claw closed on the cart handle.

Wood crunched right beside Seria's ear.

She backed away, caught her right heel on a coil of rope, and felt the world tilt.

Someone grabbed the back of her clothes. The collar pulled tight against her throat as her feet dragged across the stones.

At the same instant, a broad shield slid between her and the claw.

Bang!

The impact drove the man's left shoulder back. His boot slid half a handspan through the salt before he wedged his heel into a gap in the paving.

“Brace,” he said quietly.

A faint light spread from his shield, covering his body.

The next impact was different. His arm did not take it alone. His hips and knees lowered with the blow, seeming to take some of the force out of it.

Seria found herself close enough to see his face.

He looked to be in his mid-twenties. Sweat flattened his short black hair, and a small scar marked the outer end of his left eyebrow. His broad face and heavy eyelids made him look tired, but his brown eyes never lost track of the claw.

“Can you walk?” he asked without looking at her.

“Of course.”

He glanced at her feet.

“Not quickly,” she added.

“That's the part that matters…”

He planted both feet and angled his shield. As the claw slid aside, he turned and drove the spear in his right hand into a gap beneath the shell.

He pulled it straight back out. Only dark red slime clung to the point.

The creature raised its claw again.

He reset his shield.

“That's not going to do it…”

Seria watched the creature turn, its broken claw dragging on the stones while the good one opened toward the man. When he moved his feet, it turned after him.

“It goes toward vibrations in the ground. It's following your footsteps, not your spear.”

“Then stop moving.”

Seria realized she was still backing away.

The man tapped the paving beside him with his spear shaft. The creature leaned toward the sound.

His brown eyes flicked to her.

“Anything else?”

“The underside is soft.”

“And how do we get at it?”

Seria looked at the water below the steps. She almost told him to turn it over with a current.

A method she could no longer use herself.

While he waited for her answer, the man raised his shield again. His teeth clenched as the claw slammed into it.

“If you think of something, tell me!”

The second creature climbed the steps behind the first.

Harbor guards came running out of an alley with long hooks. The first tried to squeeze past the cart, then stopped. Its load bed lay at an angle across their way.

“Shift that cart! We need to get through!”

The guards behind him grabbed hold.

Seria moved aside for them and noticed a rope hanging beside a timber post. She followed it upward to an empty cargo net suspended from a pulley.

If she loosened the rope tied to the iron ring on the post, the net would come down. She had seen the same rig used to load heavy cargo onto ships at sea.

If I can get that under its legs…

She called to the man with the shield.

“Can we lower that net?”

He looked up.

“Undo the rope.”

“And to raise it?”

“Someone has to pull.”

The water would not move for her.

People could.

Seria headed for the post. On her second step her ankle gave way, but she caught herself on the cart's broken side. A splinter drove into her palm.

She kept going.

“Don't let it all go at once!” the man shouted behind her.

She caught the taut rope in one hand and felt for the knot with the other. Even underwater, when undoing a rescue line, she had always taken the strain before loosening the knot. Otherwise, the whole net could drop at once.

She eased the loop out of the knot and caught the released rope in both hands.

The wet fibers pressed into her injured palm. Gritting her teeth, she let the rope slip through a little at a time.

The net descended slowly.

“Bring it this way!”

“I hear you!”

The man tapped the stone beside the net with the butt of his spear. When the creature turned, he retreated a little behind his shield. Each time the claw struck, he bent his knees to hold. As soon as it lifted, he tapped the stone again.

The creature followed him onto the spread net.

Seria watched its legs, holding fast to the rope. The front legs stepped in, then the hind legs.

“Stop there!”

The man stopped and braced his shield.

Seria pulled. The edges of the net rose toward the pulley. Loose mesh drew tight beneath the legs on one side.

The creature tried to turn, but those legs no longer reached the ground.

It caught a rope in its claw and tugged, tangling two front legs in the mesh on the opposite side. The more it twisted, the more net gathered beneath it.

Seria hauled in the slack and held the line taut.

The rope burned across her palm. Her body was dragged toward the post.

“Help me!”

Her voice came out louder than she expected.

Two nearby guards seized the rope. The woman with the salt cart joined them.

Without letting go, Seria moved around the post and out of their way. The weight of three more people came onto the line.

The pulley shrieked.

One side of the creature rose. Its front legs scraped the stone while the hind legs flailed in the air.

Under the net, Seria saw a pale, soft joint exposed.

“Third leg from the front! On the inside!”

The spear went in.

The man struck and stepped aside rather than holding his ground. The upturned claw snapped shut on empty air.

The creature's feet struck the paving a few more times. Then they went slack.

On the other side of the cart, the guards had driven the second creature back down the steps with their hooks. An iron barrier dropped across the way.

Clang. Clang.

Something hard struck it twice from the far side.

Seria tried to let go of the rope.

Her hand would not open. She had to peel her fingers away one at a time.

The woman was breathing hard.

“My cart…”

Its broken handle lay on the ground. Half the salt was gone, and the rest was wet.

Seria had no idea what to say.

“I…”

“Leave it. The child's all right.”

The woman wiped her forehead with the back of her hand. She had said to leave it, but there was nothing all right about the way she looked at the cart.

The man with the spear returned. He wiped its point with an old cloth and leaned it against the post. The shield was still strapped to his left arm.

“Your hand's bleeding. Did you hurt it?”

“It's nothing.”

“Show me. Let me see how bad it is.”

Seria held out her hand.

Three red lines scored her palm.

After looking at them, he pointed to her foot. There was blood beneath her right sole too.

“That looks more like a problem…”

Strangely, hearing him say it made the foot hurt more.

He crouched. She started to step back, then stopped.

“I just want to look at the cut.”

“I know.”

“You could have fooled me.”

Seria shut her mouth.

“We'd better get you to the clinic. Walk around the docks barefoot and those cuts will get infected.”

“Is it far?”

“Not usually.”

Seria narrowed her eyes.

“It might be today,” he added.

The guards dragged away the dead creature, net and all. The bell had stopped, but people were still hurrying about. Calls asking after the injured mingled with voices counting scattered cargo.

The man picked up a short wooden pole from the side of the road, a spare shaft for one of the guards' hooks. He glanced at its owner for permission, brushed dirt off the end, and handed it to Seria. Then he retrieved his own spear from the post.

“Lean on this. Or on my shoulder, if you need to.”

She took the pole.

“What's your name?”

“Ian.”

“I'm Seria.”

Ian waited a moment. The way she stopped made it sound as though a lengthy title ought to follow.

Third Princess of Nereia remained unsaid.

She did not know what difference it would make here.

“All right, Seria. To the right.”

For a moment, she considered the unfamiliar way he had said her name.

No title attached.

Ian walked ahead, then slowed. His footsteps settled into the pace of her walking pole.

Instead of thanking him for it, she asked something else.

“Does that happen often?”

“Twice last month. Three times this week.”

Seria stopped.

A narrow drain ran beside her feet. Black water was moving between the stones.

It was not flowing out toward the sea.

It was going inland.

Toward the distant tower.

The stone path rose slightly in that direction. An incoming tide should have filled the lowest ground first. Yet this water was creeping uphill along the bottom of the channel.

The black film slid forward in one long movement, then stopped.

A moment later, it gave two short jerks, as if someone had tugged a rope.

Seria tightened her grip on the pole.

Ian turned back.

“What is it?”

“This water.”

Instinct still urged her to reach out. Her fingers moved a little, pulling at the cuts in her palm.

She lowered her hand.

She could no longer call the water to a halt. But she could still see where it was going.

“It's not coming out of there.”

She pointed at the tower.

“It's going back in.”
